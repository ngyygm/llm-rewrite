\begin{abstract}
Chinese text rewriting evaluation is difficult because a good rewrite must preserve meaning while changing expression, making source-rewrite similarity an unreliable proxy for quality.
We introduce \textbf{RewritingBench}, a diagnostic benchmark of 730 Chinese source-rewrite pairs with human quality ratings from three trained annotators (ICC\,(2,1)\,$=$\,$0.87$).
Using this benchmark, we show that seven commonly used source-similarity metrics, including lexical overlap and embedding-based similarities, correlate negatively with human scores.
This reveals a systematic mismatch: similarity metrics reward staying close to the source, while humans reward successful transformation under meaning preservation.
We then train RewriteJudge, a task-specific evaluator for Chinese rewrite quality.
Task-specific training substantially improves alignment with human rankings over zero-shot and general-purpose judges.
Cross-source pairwise aggregation achieves the highest ranking correlation in our benchmark ($\rho=0.688$).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

A rewrite should be semantically faithful but expressively different.
We focus on \emph{general-purpose Chinese text improvement rewriting}, where the goal is to improve clarity, fluency, and readability while preserving the source meaning.
This setting differs from reference matching tasks because high-quality rewrites are expected to depart from the source wording, so surface overlap is not a reasonable proxy for quality.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/ai_generated/framework_overview_v2.png}
\caption{Overview of RewritingBench: benchmark construction, diagnostic analysis of the mismatch between similarity metrics and human quality scores, cross-source pairwise preference evaluation, and preliminary model-scored filtering analysis.}
\label{fig:framework}
\end{figure}

Standard metrics such as BLEU~\citep{papineni2002bleu} and ROUGE~\citep{lin2004rouge} rely on surface overlap, while embedding similarities such as SBERT COSINE reward semantic closeness.
For rewriting, however, strong outputs often need to move away from the wording of the source.
This raises a concrete research question: \emph{when the desired output should differ from the input, are source-rewrite similarity metrics measuring quality or merely measuring similarity?}
The question is especially open for Chinese rewriting, where we are not aware of a human-rated diagnostic benchmark specifically targeting general-purpose rewrite quality despite growing interest in Chinese LLM evaluation~\citep{huang2023ceval}.

We organize this work around three research questions.
\textbf{RQ1:} Can trained annotators reliably judge general-purpose Chinese rewrite quality?
\textbf{RQ2:} Do source-rewrite similarity metrics align with human rewrite quality judgments?
\textbf{RQ3:} Does task-specific evaluator training, especially pairwise preference aggregation, improve global ranking of rewrite quality compared with direct score prediction and zero-shot judging?


To answer these questions, we present \textbf{RewritingBench} (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}), a diagnostic benchmark and analysis of Chinese rewriting evaluation.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item We introduce RewritingBench, a human-rated diagnostic benchmark for Chinese general-purpose rewriting evaluation.
\item We show that source-rewrite similarity metrics are negatively aligned with human rewrite quality, revealing a systematic mismatch between similarity and transformation quality.
\item We train RewriteJudge and show that task-specific supervision improves ranking alignment; cross-source pairwise aggregation achieves the highest ranking correlation ($\rho=0.688$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Source Similarity and Reference-Based Metrics}

Traditional rewriting evaluation relies on overlap metrics like BLEU~\citep{papineni2002bleu} and ROUGE~\citep{lin2004rouge}, while embedding metrics such as BERTScore~\citep{zhang2020bertscore}, MoverScore~\citep{zhao2019moverscore}, BARTScore~\citep{yuan2021bartscore}, and SBERT~\citep{reimers2019sbert} improve semantic matching through contextualized representations.
Methods such as ParaScore~\citep{lan2023parascore} and \citet{alam2024generalized} consider paraphrase quality and multiple dimensions, and prior surveys document broader obstacles in NLG evaluation~\citep{gehrmann2022repairing, gao2024llmbased}.
These methods mostly treat similarity as a useful proxy for quality.
We do not claim to exhaust all automatic evaluation methods; instead, we focus on a diagnostic question: whether source-rewrite similarity, a common proxy in rewriting evaluation, aligns with human quality judgments.
Our work tests this assumption in Chinese rewriting, where desirable transformation may make similarity and quality conflict.

\subsection{LLM-as-a-Judge and Task-Specific Evaluators}

The LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm offers a flexible alternative to reference metrics.
G-Eval~\citep{liu2024geval} uses prompting for structured evaluation, Prometheus~2~\citep{kim2024prometheus2} fine-tunes a 7B model for absolute and pairwise scoring, and Chatbot Arena~\citep{chiang2024chatbot} leverages human pairwise comparisons.
However, general-purpose judges can exhibit bias and rating inconsistency~\citep{wang2024vlm, wang2024notfair, lee2025reliable}.
We examine whether such judges transfer to the specialized, subjective task of Chinese rewrite quality evaluation and whether task-specific training is necessary.

\subsection{Pairwise Preference and Ranking-Based Evaluation}

Pairwise comparison is often more stable than absolute scoring because it asks for a relative judgment rather than global calibration.
\citet{kim2024prometheus2} find higher agreement with human judgments in general NLP tasks, \citet{zheng2023judging} use pairwise comparison for LLM assessment, and work on generative selection shows that comparative scoring can improve selection from $N$ candidates~\citep{wang2024genrm}.
Preference learning work further shows that models can learn from pairwise signals without explicit reward scores~\citep{rafailov2023dpo, lambert2024rewardbench}.
We test whether this comparative signal specifically helps Chinese rewriting in a cross-source global ranking setting, where human quality judgments are subjective and ordinal.

\section{Benchmark and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Task definition.} Given a Chinese source text $s$ and a candidate rewrite $r$, the evaluator should judge whether $r$ improves expression while preserving the source meaning.
A high-quality rewrite should be faithful, fluent, natural, and appropriately transformed.
Transformation is valuable only when it improves clarity, readability, or expression; unnecessary changes that distort meaning or reduce naturalness should be penalized.
Our benchmark focuses on \emph{general-purpose text improvement rewrites} that enhance fluency, clarity, or readability without targeting a specific style.
Formally, the absolute scoring formulation is:
\begin{equation}
    f: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{R} \rightarrow [0, 5]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:eval_framework}

We define text rewriting quality along four complementary dimensions, scored from 0 to 5: \textbf{Meaning preservation} (preserve the source meaning without factual errors or omissions of key content), \textbf{Expression improvement} (improve fluency, clarity, and readability), \textbf{Appropriate transformation} (make necessary and effective structural, phrasing, and syntactic adjustments, rather than mechanical substitution or excessive rewriting), and \textbf{Naturalness and idiomaticity} (ensure the Chinese expression is natural and contextually appropriate).
Since RewritingBench focuses on general-purpose rewriting rather than a predefined target style, we use expression improvement instead of style fidelity as a core criterion.
The final score is a holistic judgment integrating all dimensions.
In our protocol, annotators provide only this unified rating, using the four dimensions as internal guidelines rather than separate scores.
We do not reward divergence for its own sake: a rewrite that is lexically or structurally different but semantically distorted or less natural should receive a low score.

\subsection{Benchmark Construction}

We collected 730 Chinese rewrite pairs, each consisting of one source text and one generated rewrite.
Each source text appears once in the benchmark (730 unique inputs), and the training and evaluation split has no exact source overlap.
Source texts contain 49 to 251 Chinese characters (mean 103.2, median 93), while rewrites contain 20 to 247 characters (mean 93.4, median 84.5).
Three trained annotators independently scored each pair on the holistic scale from 0 to 5.
Agreement is high across multiple statistics: mean pairwise Spearman $\rho = 0.88$, ICC(2,1)\,$=$\,$0.87$, Krippendorff's alpha (ordinal)\,$=$\,$0.87$, mean weighted kappa (quadratic)\,$=$\,$0.87$, exact agreement\,$=$\,$81.1\%$, and $\pm$1 agreement\,$=$\,$94.1\%$.
We use the average rating as the gold standard.
The dataset is split into 600 training pairs and 130 evaluation pairs (129 after removing one parsing issue) using stratified sampling by score.
The evaluation set closely reflects the full score distribution: 0 (15.5\%), 1 (27.9\%), 2 (24.8\%), 3 (20.2\%), 4 (9.3\%), 5 (2.3\%).
Because the current version does not contain complete source-domain and generator metadata, we treat RewritingBench as a diagnostic benchmark rather than a comprehensive coverage benchmark.

\subsection{Class Balanced Training Strategy}
\label{sec:balanced}

The score distribution is imbalanced (Score~0: 15\%, Score~5: 3\%).
When we train RewriteJudge on the original data, 82\% of its predictions collapse to Score~1 and the correlation with human scores is low ($\rho=0.253$).
We therefore oversample minority score classes and create a balanced training set of 1,008 samples (168 per class).
After balancing, the model predicts across the full score range and better matches human rankings.

\subsection{Evaluator Training}
\label{sec:lora}

We fine-tune Qwen3 8B~\citep{yang2025qwen3} and Qwen2.5 7B Instruct~\citep{qwen2025qwen25} with LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}.
For absolute scoring, the model outputs a single holistic score from 0 to 5.
For pairwise preference learning, the model chooses which of two rewrites is higher quality, and comparison outcomes are aggregated into win rate rankings.
Full training details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_training}.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}

All correlations are computed on a held out set of 129 samples to prevent data leakage. We evaluate performance against human gold standards using \textbf{Spearman $\rho$} as the primary ranking metric, \textbf{Pearson $r$}, and \textbf{Kendall $\tau$}. We also report \textbf{MAE / RMSE}, and \textbf{Exact / $\pm$1 / $\pm$2} agreement percentages to measure absolute error and prediction tolerance.


\subsubsection{Baselines}

We evaluate 19 methods across four categories: \textbf{traditional metrics} (BLEU, ROUGE L, SBERT COSINE, TFIDF COSINE, W2V COSINE, JACCARD WORD, JACCARD CHAR), \textbf{zero-shot LLMs} (Qwen3 and Qwen2.5, 7B to 14B), \textbf{general-purpose evaluators} (G-Eval, Prometheus 2), and \textbf{fine-tuned variants} (LoRA with different data sizes and formats). For pairwise evaluation, we additionally test eight zero-shot models from 72B to endpoint-scale, including Qwen2.5 72B Instruct, Qwen3 235B A22B, DeepSeek V3 series, GPT 4.1, and Kimi K2.

\subsection{RQ1/RQ2: Alignment with Human Judgment}
\label{sec:main_results}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} summarizes the benchmark evaluation.
For RQ1, the high annotator agreement reported in Section~\ref{sec:method} establishes that trained humans can reliably judge Chinese rewrite quality.
For RQ2, the results show a sharp divide: task-specific fine-tuning can recover a meaningful signal (RewriteJudge: $\rho=0.645$), while source-similarity metrics and general-purpose LLM judges do not match the same human scores.

\textbf{Source-similarity metrics are negatively aligned with human rewrite quality.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:method_comparison_traditional}, all seven source-similarity metrics show a negative correlation with human judgment, from $\rho=-0.28$ (SBERT COSINE) to $\rho=-0.60$ (JACCARD CHAR), with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals that do not cross zero.
This occurs because quality rewrites often diverge from the source wording, which overlap metrics penalize.
These metrics are not designed to evaluate transformation quality: the negative correlations show that using source similarity as a proxy for rewrite quality can systematically rank copy-like rewrites above successful rewrites.
Even SBERT COSINE ($\rho=-0.28$) fails because semantic similarity alone does not distinguish between faithful transformation and mere copying.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{figures/qwen3-8b_and_qwen2.5-7b/method_comparison_traditional.pdf}
\caption{Source-similarity metrics are negatively aligned with human rewrite quality. All seven metrics show negative Spearman $\rho$ with bootstrap 95\% CIs that do not cross zero.}
\label{fig:method_comparison_traditional}

\textbf{Why the failure is systematic.}
The negative correlations are not random metric noise; they follow from two recurring error patterns (detailed examples in Appendix~\ref{sec:error_analysis}).
\textit{Surface similarity without quality}: rewrites that are close copies receive high overlap scores because they preserve source wording, but humans assign low scores because little rewriting occurred.
\textit{Quality without surface similarity}: strong rewrites that restructure syntax and vary vocabulary receive low overlap scores, even when they preserve meaning and improve fluency.
Thus, overlap metrics optimize source similarity, whereas human judgment rewards transformation that preserves meaning.
For rewriting, these objectives can be inversely correlated.

\textbf{General-purpose LLM evaluators underperform in this setup.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:method_comparison_llm}, we tested Prometheus~2~\citep{kim2024prometheus2} ($\rho=0.145$) and G-Eval~\citep{liu2024geval} ($\rho=0.041$), as well as zero-shot Qwen3 14B ($\rho=0.129$).
All are below our fine-tuned 8B model.
This shows that general-purpose evaluator tuning does not transfer reliably to Chinese rewrite quality assessment without task-specific training.
This result does not mean that RewriteJudge is a general-purpose rewriting evaluator; it shows that task-specific supervision is necessary for this benchmark and task definition.

\section{Cross-Source Pairwise Ranking for Rewrite Quality}
\label{sec:pairwise}
% ============================================================

RQ3 asks which learning signal best matches human rewrite quality judgments.
Absolute scoring requires the model to place each candidate on a global scale from 0 to 5, whereas pairwise preference evaluation asks a simpler comparative question: which of two rewrites is better?
Figure~\ref{fig:paradigm_comparison} illustrates how the same rewriting case is processed under these two paradigms.
In our current benchmark, each source has one rewrite, so pairwise preference is constructed across different source texts rather than among multiple rewrites of the same source.

\subsection{Pairwise Construction and Aggregation}

We create a global quality ranking set from the 600 training samples.
For each pair, the two rewrites must differ by at least 0.67 points in human score:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P} = \{(r_i, r_j) \mid |q_i - q_j| \geq 0.67\}
    \label{eq:pairs}
\end{equation}
yielding 2,652 balanced comparisons (1,326 per class).
Because the paired rewrites come from different source texts, each training example presents both the source and rewrite for candidate A and the source and rewrite for candidate B.
The model then chooses which candidate demonstrates higher overall rewrite quality relative to its own source.

At inference, all $\binom{129}{2}=8{,}256$ evaluation pairs are scored; each rewrite's \emph{win rate} is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{WinRate}(r_i) = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{1}[\text{Pref}(r_i, r_j) = 1]
    \label{eq:winrate}
\end{equation}
and Spearman correlation is computed between win rates and human average scores.

\subsection{RQ3 Results}

Table~\ref{tab:pairwise_results} compares absolute scoring and pairwise preference learning against zero-shot LLM baselines.
The pairwise model achieves $\rho=0.688$, a 0.043 absolute Spearman gain over absolute scoring ($\rho=0.645$).
Because no significance test is performed on this difference, we do not claim statistical superiority.
We interpret this as a consistent improvement from pairwise preference aggregation, though no significance test is performed on this difference.
Among zero-shot models, Qwen2.5 72B ($\rho=0.406$) and DeepSeek V3 ($\rho=0.391$) show moderate positive correlation, while several larger or newer models perform poorly with the same prompt: Qwen3 235B ($\rho=0.132$), DeepSeek V3.1 ($\rho=-0.049$), DeepSeek V3.2 ($\rho=-0.274$), GPT-4.1 ($\rho=-0.145$), and Kimi K2 ($\rho=-0.472$).
These results suggest that task-specific supervision is more reliable than model scale alone for this evaluation problem.

\subsection{Zero Shot Model Scale and Ranking Correlation}
\label{sec:scaling}

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} plots parameter count against Spearman $\rho$ for all pairwise zero-shot baselines.
In our zero-shot prompting setup, larger model scale alone does not reliably improve rewriting evaluation.
We do not interpret this as a general scaling law, since model families, training recipes, and prompting interfaces differ.

Within model families, newer or larger endpoints do not reliably outperform older ones in our zero-shot prompting setup (e.g., Qwen3 235B at $\rho$=0.132 vs.\ Qwen2.5 72B at $\rho$=0.406), though these systems differ in training and interfaces so this should not be read as a general generational claim (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_acc_rho} for within-family comparisons).

\textbf{Cross-source vs.\ same-source preference.}
Under the Qwen2.5 7B setting, the pairwise model trained on global cross-source comparisons reaches $\rho=0.665$, while the model trained on generated pairs from the same source reaches $\rho=0.421$.
The stronger cross-source result should be interpreted carefully.
Cross-source comparisons pair rewrites from different source texts with different human scores and therefore cover a wider quality range, but they are less directly aligned with the practical setting of choosing among multiple rewrites for the same input.
In our preliminary same-source setting, performance is lower, suggesting that same-source preference remains an open challenge rather than a solved problem.
Pairwise evaluation for multiple rewrites of the same source remains an important setting for future data collection.

\textbf{Why can pairwise help absolute scoring?}
We attribute this to two factors:
(1) pairwise comparison may provide an easier local learning signal than direct score calibration, although cross-source comparison still requires a global notion of quality;
(2) win rate aggregation provides a more robust quality estimate than any single absolute prediction, as each rewrite's score is informed by multiple comparisons.
This improvement comes at the cost of $O(n^2)$ comparisons at inference.

\section{Robustness and Bias Analysis}
\label{sec:robustness}

The previous sections establish the core scientific claim: source-similarity metrics are negatively aligned with human rewrite quality, and task-specific supervision with comparative signals can help recover rankings that match human scores.
We next summarize robustness checks and bias analysis.

\subsection{Training Robustness}

Training on the imbalanced score distribution causes mode collapse (Qwen3 8B: $\rho=0.337$), while class-balanced oversampling (168 per score class) improves performance to $\rho=0.645$, confirming that rewrite evaluation requires supervision across the full quality range.
Rubric-aligned prompts improve absolute scoring ($\rho=0.645$ vs.\ $\rho=0.607$ standard), pairwise preference is numerically higher ($\rho=0.688$), and reasoning prefixes hurt ($\rho=0.360$).
Additional ablations are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_pairwise_ablation}.

\subsection{Bias Checks: Length, Verbosity, and Position Effects}

A reliable evaluator should judge quality based on content, not spurious features such as text length or verbosity.
Table~\ref{tab:bias_analysis} compares bias across six methods along four dimensions.
Both RewriteJudge variants show minimal output length bias (Qwen3 8B: $r=-0.033$; Qwen2.5 7B: $r=-0.058$), well below zero-shot Qwen3 8B ($r=0.325$), character overlap ($r=0.306$), and a length heuristic ($r=0.287$).
RewriteJudge Qwen3 8B achieves near-zero verbosity bias ($\rho=0.006$), far below zero-shot Qwen3 8B ($\rho=0.382$) and character overlap ($\rho=0.760$).

RewriteJudge maintains low position bias (Qwen3 8B: $r=0.120$; Qwen2.5 7B: $r=0.187$), while zero-shot Qwen3 8B shows higher bias ($r=0.437$).
Char Overlap and Length Heuristic are non-pairwise methods where position bias is not defined.
These results confirm that RewriteJudge's ranking alignment with human scores is not driven by spurious length or verbosity features.
Extended bias analysis with additional figures is provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:bias}.

As a preliminary downstream application, RewriteJudge can be used to filter generated rewrite candidates.
Selecting the top 50\% by model score yields higher model-assigned quality than random selection, while BLEU-based filtering performs close to random, consistent with the negative alignment found in Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic}.
Because this analysis uses model-assigned scores rather than independent human ratings, full results are deferred to Appendix~\ref{sec:downstream_results}.


% ============================================================
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

Source similarity metrics reward outputs that stay close to the input wording; for rewriting, this is systematically misleading because a high-quality rewrite must both preserve meaning and transform expression, and these objectives can be inversely correlated.
The negative correlations observed in RewritingBench are not random noise but follow from this fundamental mismatch in objectives.
This implication extends beyond Chinese to any generation setting where good outputs should differ from the input while preserving intent.

RewriteJudge substantially improves ranking alignment over zero-shot and general-purpose evaluators, and bias analysis confirms it is not driven by length or verbosity features.
However, this result demonstrates that task-specific supervision helps for this benchmark and task definition, not that RewriteJudge generalizes as a universal rewriting evaluator.
Cross-source pairwise aggregation achieves the highest correlation ($\rho=0.688$), but applies to global ranking rather than same-source selection and comes at $O(n^2)$ cost.
Pairwise accuracy alone is also insufficient: Qwen3 235B reaches 72.0\% pairwise accuracy yet only $\rho=0.132$, while our fine-tuned 8B model achieves $\rho=0.688$ (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_acc_rho}).

\textbf{Scope and generalization.}
RewritingBench contains 730 Chinese general-purpose improvement pairs from a single-rewrite-per-source design; generalization to other languages, rewriting subtasks (style transfer, simplification), and same-source candidate selection remains unknown.
The cross-source pairwise evaluation covers a wide quality range but requires a global notion of quality across different inputs and is not directly aligned with the practical setting of choosing among rewrites for the same source.

\textbf{Validation and calibration.}
Our analysis focuses on ranking quality; better-calibrated numeric scores are an open direction.
The filtering experiment (Appendix~\ref{sec:downstream_results}) uses model-assigned scores rather than independent human ratings, so filtering gains should not be interpreted as human-validated quality improvements.
Full-parameter tuning and larger annotated datasets may further improve performance beyond the LoRA adaptation used here.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We introduced RewritingBench, a diagnostic benchmark for Chinese general-purpose rewrite quality evaluation.
The benchmark shows that source-rewrite similarity can be negatively aligned with human judgments because high-quality rewriting requires both meaning preservation and appropriate expression transformation.
Task-specific RewriteJudge training substantially improves ranking alignment compared with source-similarity metrics and zero-shot judges, and cross-source pairwise aggregation achieves the highest ranking correlation ($\rho=0.688$).
However, same-source preference evaluation, calibrated scoring, and human-validated filtering remain open directions.
Overall, our results suggest that Chinese rewriting evaluation should measure transformation quality under semantic faithfulness rather than source similarity alone.

\section{Training Configuration Details}
\label{sec:appendix_training}

Table~\ref{tab:training_config} summarizes the training configuration for \textsc{RewriteJudge}. We employ Low Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to fine-tune the model, injecting trainable matrices into seven target modules: query, key, value, output projection, and the three MLP layers (gate, up, and down projections). This configuration results in trainable parameters totaling approximately 0.2\% of the full model.

For a pretrained weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, LoRA reparameterizes the update as:
\begin{equation}
W' = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA
\label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}
where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, and $r \ll \min(d, k)$.
Only $A$ and $B$ are trained while $W_0$ remains frozen.

\subsection{Hyperparameters}
The LoRA adapters are configured with rank $r=16$, scaling factor $\alpha=32$, and a dropout rate of 0.05. We fine-tune the model for 3 epochs using a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-4}$ with a cosine decay schedule (3\% warmup). The training uses bf16 mixed precision. With a batch size of 4 and 4 gradient accumulation steps (effective batch size 16), the total training time is approximately 16 minutes on a single NVIDIA H20 GPU.

\section{Additional Error Analysis: Why Traditional Metrics Fail}
\label{sec:error_analysis}
% ============================================================

Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic} summarizes the core error mechanism in the main text.
Here we provide additional detail on the two recurring patterns behind the negative correlation between overlap metrics and human judgment.

\subsection{Two Error Patterns}

We identify two systematic error patterns that explain the negative correlations:

\textbf{Pattern 1: Surface similarity without quality.}
Rewrites that are close copies of the source text, changing only a few words while preserving the original structure, receive high overlap metric scores but low human ratings.
For example, a rewrite that substitutes ``\textit{加盟}'' with ``\textit{加盟后}'' and ``\textit{场场}'' with ``\textit{几乎每场}'' achieves BLEU=0.55 but receives a human score of only 0.7.
The metric rewards lexical overlap that reflects copying, not genuine rewrite quality.

\textbf{Pattern 2: Quality without surface similarity.}
Strong rewrites that restructure the sentence, using different vocabulary and syntax while preserving meaning, receive very low metric scores but high human ratings.
A rewrite that restructures a passage about a press conference into a more concise summary achieves BLEU=0.00 but receives a human score of 4.0.
The metric penalizes the very lexical variation that characterizes successful rewriting.

\subsection{Root Cause: Misaligned Objectives}

These two patterns show that overlap metrics and human ratings answer different questions: overlap metrics measure how \emph{similar} a rewrite is to its source, while human judgment evaluates how \emph{good} the rewrite is.
The critical insight is that these are not merely different metrics; they are \emph{inversely correlated} for rewriting.
Pattern~1 shows that high source similarity indicates \emph{insufficient transformation} (the rewrite barely changed anything), while Pattern~2 shows that high source dissimilarity indicates \emph{successful transformation} (the rewrite meaningfully transformed the text).
This inverse relationship explains why the negative correlations are universal and statistically significant rather than reflecting random noise or a specific metric deficiency.
For text rewriting, a task where the goal is to differ from the source while preserving meaning, these two objectives are inversely correlated.
This explains why the strongest negative correlations are observed for character level metrics (JACCARD CHAR: $\rho$=$-$0.60), which most directly measure surface overlap, while semantic metrics like SBERT COSINE ($\rho$=$-$0.28) show somewhat weaker, but still negative, correlations.

\subsection{Implications}

This finding has practical implications for Chinese rewriting evaluation: source-rewrite similarity metrics are unreliable proxies for rewrite quality in our benchmark, and task-specific evaluators trained on human-rated data provide a more aligned alternative.

请帮我判断输入文本与输出文本是否满足以下要求：
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{要求1.} 在保持原文含义不变的前提下，用原文不同的文字进行表达，不额外新增、删减会严重影响语义的内容。
  \item \textbf{要求2.} 在保证逻辑正确、语序通顺的前提下，变换实体、短语、概念出现的前后顺序、结构关系；顺序变换幅度越小，分数越低。
  \item \textbf{要求3.} 在保证特殊实体、引用、解释、特定语句不变的前提下，使用同义词替换；同义词替换越少，分数越低。
  \item \textbf{要求4.} 保持与原文语言风格一致，字数长度变化合理；语言风格差距越大，分数越低。
  \item \textbf{要求5.} 改写综合评分要非常严格，同时考虑上述4个要求；在保证语义一致（要求1）的前提下，（要求2,3）变化越大越好，字数越接近越好（要求4）；如果存在大量长字段完全复制，则给0分。
\end{itemize}

\medskip
评分准则，每个要求最低0分，满分均为5分，评分要尽可能严格。

\textit{Please evaluate whether the output text satisfies the following requirements with respect to the input text:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textit{\textbf{Req.\,1.} Express the same meaning as the original using different wording, without adding or omitting content that significantly affects semantics.}
  \item \textit{\textbf{Req.\,2.} Rearrange the order and structural relations of entities, phrases, and concepts while ensuring logical correctness and fluency; the smaller the reordering, the lower the score.}
  \item \textit{\textbf{Req.\,3.} Replace words with synonyms while keeping special entities, citations, explanations, and fixed expressions unchanged; fewer synonym substitutions yield a lower score.}
  \item \textit{\textbf{Req.\,4.} Maintain a language style consistent with the original and a reasonable change in length; the greater the stylistic divergence, the lower the score.}
  \item \textit{\textbf{Req.\,5.} Apply strict holistic scoring that simultaneously considers all four requirements above. Given semantic consistency (Req.\,1), maximize structural and lexical changes (Reqs.\,2 and 3) while keeping length close to the original (Req.\,4). If large verbatim spans are copied, assign a score of 0.}
\end{itemize}

\medskip\textit{Scoring guideline: each requirement is scored from 0 (minimum) to 5 (maximum); scoring should be as strict as possible.}
