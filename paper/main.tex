\begin{abstract}
Evaluating Chinese text rewriting is inherently subjective: a good rewrite must preserve meaning while improving expression, and annotators often make relative rather than calibrated absolute judgments.
This raises a central question: what supervision signal should be used to train LLM-based rewrite judges? We compare absolute-score supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with pairwise preference training for Chinese rewrite quality evaluation.
Using 730 human-rated Chinese source--rewrite pairs from three trained annotators (ICC\,(2,1)\,$=$\,$0.87$), we construct pairwise preference supervision from holistic human ratings and train RewriteJudge to compare and rank rewrites.
Pairwise preference training achieves the highest ranking alignment ($\rho=0.688$), outperforming absolute-score SFT ($\rho=0.645$) and substantially exceeding zero-shot and general-purpose LLM judges.
Data efficiency analysis shows that pairwise supervision maintains stable performance with only 25\% of training data.
We further show that source-similarity metrics align negatively with human judgments, confirming that rewrite evaluation requires learning human preferences rather than measuring source overlap.
Our results suggest that contrastive preference supervision is a more suitable training paradigm for subjective rewriting evaluation than absolute-score calibration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

A rewrite should be semantically faithful but expressively different.
We focus on \emph{general-purpose Chinese text improvement rewriting}, where the goal is to improve clarity, fluency, and readability while preserving the source meaning.
Evaluating such subjective transformations is challenging: annotators often make relative quality judgments rather than calibrated absolute scores, and standard metrics such as BLEU~\citep{papineni2002bleu} and ROUGE~\citep{lin2004rouge} rely on source overlap, which can be negatively aligned with rewrite quality.
This raises a central question: \emph{how should we train LLM-based judges for subjective rewriting evaluation, where absolute score calibration is difficult but relative preference is more natural?}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{figures/ai_generated/framework_overview_v2.png}
\caption{Overview of our approach: we collect human-rated Chinese rewrite pairs, construct preference supervision from holistic ratings, and train RewriteJudge via contrastive learning with win-rate aggregation.}
\label{fig:framework}
\end{figure}

We study two training paradigms for LLM-based rewrite judges: \emph{absolute-score SFT}, which trains the model to predict a 0--5 score directly, and \emph{pairwise preference training}, which trains the model to compare candidate rewrites and aggregates pairwise preferences into a global ranking.
Using 730 human-rated Chinese source--rewrite pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}), we show that pairwise preference training better aligns with human rankings than absolute-score SFT and substantially outperforms zero-shot and general-purpose LLM judges.
We further provide diagnostic evidence that source-similarity metrics are negatively aligned with human judgments, motivating the need for learned preference-based evaluation.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item We formulate Chinese rewriting evaluation as a subjective preference-alignment problem, where relative quality judgments are more natural than calibrated absolute scores.
\item We propose RewriteJudge, a pairwise preference training framework that converts human holistic ratings into comparison supervision and aggregates model preferences into win-rate rankings.
\item We show that pairwise preference training achieves stronger alignment with human rankings than absolute-score SFT and substantially outperforms zero-shot and general-purpose LLM judges; diagnostic analyses further explain why source similarity is an unreliable proxy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

Traditional rewriting evaluation relies on overlap metrics like BLEU~\citep{papineni2002bleu} and ROUGE~\citep{lin2004rouge}, while embedding metrics such as BERTScore~\citep{zhang2020bertscore}, MoverScore~\citep{zhao2019moverscore}, BARTScore~\citep{yuan2021bartscore}, and SBERT~\citep{reimers2019sbert} improve semantic matching through contextualized representations.
Methods such as ParaScore~\citep{lan2023parascore} and \citet{alam2024generalized} consider paraphrase quality and multiple dimensions, and prior surveys document broader obstacles in NLG evaluation~\citep{gehrmann2022repairing, gao2024llmbased}.
These methods mostly treat similarity as a useful proxy for quality.
We do not claim to exhaust all automatic evaluation methods; instead, we focus on a diagnostic question: whether source-rewrite similarity, a common proxy in rewriting evaluation, aligns with human quality judgments.

The LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm offers a flexible alternative to reference metrics.
G-Eval~\citep{liu2024geval} uses prompting for structured evaluation, Prometheus~2~\citep{kim2024prometheus2} fine-tunes a 7B model for absolute and pairwise scoring, and Chatbot Arena~\citep{chiang2024chatbot} leverages human pairwise comparisons.
However, general-purpose judges can exhibit bias and rating inconsistency~\citep{wang2024vlm, wang2024notfair, lee2025reliable}.
We examine whether such judges transfer to the specialized, subjective task of Chinese rewrite quality evaluation and whether task-specific training is necessary.

Pairwise comparison is often more stable than absolute scoring because it asks for a relative judgment rather than global calibration.
\citet{kim2024prometheus2} find higher agreement with human judgments in general NLP tasks, \citet{zheng2023judging} use pairwise comparison for LLM assessment, and work on generative selection shows that comparative scoring can improve selection from $N$ candidates~\citep{wang2024genrm}.
Preference learning work further shows that models can learn from pairwise signals without explicit reward scores~\citep{rafailov2023dpo, lambert2024rewardbench}.
Prior work has studied LLM judges and pairwise evaluation, but less is known about which supervision signal is better for training task-specific judges in Chinese rewriting evaluation.

\section{Preference-Based Rewrite Judge}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================

Given a Chinese source text $s$ and a candidate rewrite $r$, the evaluator should judge whether $r$ improves expression while preserving the source meaning.
A high-quality rewrite should be faithful, fluent, natural, and appropriately transformed.
We study two paradigms for training LLM-based judges (Figure~\ref{fig:paradigm_comparison}).

\subsection{Human Preference Normalization}
\label{sec:data}

We define text rewriting quality along four complementary dimensions, scored from 0 to 5: \textbf{Meaning preservation} (preserve the source meaning without factual errors or omissions of key content), \textbf{Expression improvement} (improve fluency, clarity, and readability), \textbf{Appropriate transformation} (make necessary and effective structural, phrasing, and syntactic adjustments, rather than mechanical substitution or excessive rewriting), and \textbf{Naturalness and idiomaticity} (ensure the Chinese expression is natural and contextually appropriate).
The final score is a holistic judgment integrating all dimensions; annotators provide only a unified rating, using the four dimensions as internal guidelines.
We do not reward divergence for its own sake: a rewrite that is lexically or structurally different but semantically distorted or less natural receive a low score.

We collected 730 Chinese rewrite pairs (730 unique source texts).
Source texts contain 49 to 251 Chinese characters (mean 103.2, median 93), while rewrites contain 20 to 247 characters (mean 93.4, median 84.5).
Three trained annotators independently scored each pair on the holistic scale from 0 to 5.
Agreement is high across multiple statistics: mean pairwise Spearman $\rho = 0.88$, ICC(2,1)\,$=$\,$0.87$, Krippendorff's alpha (ordinal)\,$=$\,$0.87$, mean weighted kappa (quadratic)\,$=$\,$0.87$, exact agreement\,$=$\,$81.1\%$, and $\pm$1 agreement\,$=$\,$94.1\%$.
We use the average rating as the gold standard.
The dataset is split into 600 training pairs and 130 evaluation pairs using stratified sampling by score.
The evaluation set closely reflects the full score distribution: 0 (15.5\%), 1 (27.9\%), 2 (24.8\%), 3 (20.2\%), 4 (9.3\%), 5 (2.3\%).
Because the score distribution is imbalanced, we oversample minority score classes and create a balanced training set of 1,008 samples (168 per class).
Without balancing, 82\% of predictions collapse to Score~1 ($\rho=0.253$); after balancing, the model predicts across the full range.

\subsection{Absolute Scoring and Pairwise Preference Training}
\label{sec:training}

\textbf{Absolute-score SFT.}
The standard approach trains a model to predict a holistic quality score:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\theta}: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{R} \rightarrow [0, 5]
    \label{eq:absolute}
\end{equation}
The training objective minimizes the discrepancy between predicted and human-assigned scores:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} = \mathbb{E}_{(s,r,q)} \left[ \ell(f_\theta(s,r),\, q) \right]
    \label{eq:sft_loss}
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the human quality rating and $\ell$ is a cross-entropy loss over discrete score classes.

\textbf{Pairwise preference training.}
Rather than predicting absolute scores, we train the model to compare pairs of rewrites:
\begin{equation}
    g_{\theta}: (\mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{R})^2 \rightarrow \{0, 1\}
    \label{eq:pairwise}
\end{equation}
Given two source--rewrite pairs $(s_i, r_i)$ and $(s_j, r_j)$ with human ratings $q_i > q_j$, the model learns:
\begin{equation}
\scalebox{0.95}{$
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}} = \mathbb{E}_{(r_i, r_j)} \left[ -\log P_\theta(r_i \succ r_j \mid q_i > q_j) \right]
    \label{eq:pair_loss}
$}
\end{equation}
The key difference is that absolute scoring requires global calibration across all quality levels, while pairwise training only requires local relative judgments.
For subjective tasks where annotators naturally make comparative rather than calibrated judgments, pairwise supervision provides a more natural learning signal.

We fine-tune Qwen3 8B~\citep{yang2025qwen3} and Qwen2.5 7B Instruct~\citep{qwen2025qwen25} with LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}.
For a pretrained weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, LoRA reparameterizes the update as:
\begin{equation}
W' = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA
\label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}
where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, and $r \ll \min(d, k)$.
Only $A$ and $B$ are trained while $W_0$ remains frozen.
We inject trainable matrices into seven target modules: query, key, value, output projection, and the three MLP layers (gate, up, and down projections), resulting in trainable parameters totaling approximately 0.2\% of the full model.
For absolute scoring, the model outputs a single holistic score from 0 to 5 using a rubric-grounded prompt.
We compare three prompt variants: \textbf{Simple} (minimal instructions), \textbf{Standard} (brief criteria), and \textbf{Detailed} (matching the human annotation rubric; see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_prompts}).
Rubric-aligned prompts improve absolute scoring ($\rho=0.645$ vs.\ $\rho=0.607$ for Standard), and reasoning prefixes hurt performance ($\rho=0.360$).
For pairwise preference learning, the model receives both source--rewrite pairs and chooses which candidate demonstrates higher quality.




\subsection{Win-Rate Ranking}
\label{sec:winrate_section}

From the 600 training samples, we construct pairwise comparisons for preference training.
For each pair, the two rewrites must differ by at least 0.67 points in human score:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P} = \{(r_i, r_j) \mid |q_i - q_j| \geq 0.67\}
    \label{eq:pairs}
\end{equation}
yielding 2,652 balanced comparisons (1,326 per class).
Because each source has one rewrite, pairs are constructed across different source texts.
The model receives both source--rewrite pairs and chooses which demonstrates higher overall quality relative to its own source.

At inference, all $\binom{129}{2}=8{,}256$ evaluation pairs are scored; each rewrite's \emph{win rate} is:
\begin{equation}
\scalebox{0.9}{$
    \text{WinRate}(r_i) = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{1}[\text{Pref}(r_i, r_j) = 1]
    \label{eq:winrate}
    $}
\end{equation}
Spearman correlation is then computed between win rates and human average scores.
This aggregation provides a more robust quality estimate than any single absolute prediction, as each rewrite's ranking is informed by multiple comparisons.
Because each source currently has one rewrite, our main pairwise setting is cross-source global quality ranking rather than same-source candidate selection.

\subsection{Why Pairwise Preference Helps}

\textbf{Pairwise supervision is data-efficient.}
Table~\ref{tab:data_efficiency} shows pairwise performance with different training data fractions.
Qwen3 8B reaches $\rho=0.611$ with only 25\% of the data (663 pairs) and $\rho=0.669$ with 50\%, demonstrating that pairwise supervision is data-efficient and maintains stable performance even with limited training data.
The Qwen2.5 7B result at 50\% reveals a failure mode specific to pairwise evaluation: the model develops a strong preference for the second presented candidate, which collapses the win-rate distribution after order averaging.
Position swap averaging mitigates this bias by presenting each pair in both orderings; a model that always predicts the second rewrite as better produces average preference $\approx 0.5$ per pair, collapsing the win rate distribution and yielding near-zero Spearman correlation.
This result shows that pairwise training is not merely a way to create more training examples; it also requires explicit control of position bias.
With the full comparison set, the same model recovers to $\rho=0.665$, suggesting that sufficient preference coverage and order balancing are both necessary for stable ranking.

\subsection{Robustness and Diagnostic Checks}

\textbf{Bias analysis.}
A reliable evaluator should judge quality based on content, not spurious features such as text length or verbosity.
Table~\ref{tab:bias_analysis} compares bias across methods.
Both RewriteJudge variants show minimal output length bias (Qwen3 8B: $r=-0.033$; Qwen2.5 7B: $r=-0.058$), well below zero-shot Qwen3 8B ($r=0.325$), character overlap ($r=0.306$), and a length heuristic ($r=0.287$).
RewriteJudge Qwen3 8B achieves near-zero verbosity bias ($\rho=0.006$), far below zero-shot Qwen3 8B ($\rho=0.382$) and character overlap ($\rho=0.760$).
Position bias measures candidate-order preference in pairwise evaluation; RewriteJudge maintains low position bias (Qwen3 8B: $r=0.120$; Qwen2.5 7B: $r=0.187$), while zero-shot Qwen3 8B shows higher bias ($r=0.437$).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We studied how to train LLM-based judges for Chinese text rewriting evaluation, comparing absolute-score SFT with pairwise preference training.
Using 730 human-rated Chinese source--rewrite pairs, we showed that pairwise preference training achieves the highest ranking alignment ($\rho=0.688$), outperforming absolute-score SFT ($\rho=0.645$) and substantially exceeding zero-shot and general-purpose LLM judges.
Analyses show that pairwise supervision is data-efficient, produces better ranking consistency beyond raw pairwise accuracy, and exhibits low bias across length, verbosity, and position dimensions.
Future work should investigate same-source candidate selection, larger human validation, and calibrated score prediction.


% ============================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ============================================================

Our dataset contains 730 Chinese general-purpose improvement pairs from a single-rewrite-per-source design; generalization to other languages, rewriting subtasks (style transfer, simplification), and same-source candidate selection remains unknown.

Cross-source pairwise aggregation achieves the highest correlation ($\rho=0.688$), but applies to global ranking rather than same-source selection and comes at $O(n^2)$ cost.

Our analysis focuses on ranking quality; better-calibrated numeric scores are an open direction.
Full-parameter tuning and larger annotated datasets may further improve performance beyond the LoRA adaptation used here.
This result demonstrates that task-specific supervision helps for this benchmark and task definition, not that RewriteJudge generalizes as a universal rewriting evaluator.

\section{Source-Similarity Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error_analysis}

Section~\ref{sec:experiments} summarizes the core error mechanism in the main text.
Here we provide additional detail on the two recurring patterns behind the negative correlation between overlap metrics and human judgment.

\textbf{Pattern 1: Surface similarity without quality.}
Rewrites that are close copies of the source text, changing only a few words while preserving the original structure, receive high overlap metric scores but low human ratings.
For example, a rewrite that substitutes ``\textit{加盟}'' with ``\textit{加盟后}'' and ``\textit{场场}'' with ``\textit{几乎每场}'' achieves BLEU=0.55 but receives a human score of only 0.7.
The metric rewards lexical overlap that reflects copying, not genuine rewrite quality.

\textbf{Pattern 2: Quality without surface similarity.}
Strong rewrites that restructure the sentence, using different vocabulary and syntax while preserving meaning, receive very low metric scores but high human ratings.
A rewrite that restructures a passage about a press conference into a more concise summary achieves BLEU=0.00 but receives a human score of 4.0.
The metric penalizes the very lexical variation that characterizes successful rewriting.

These two patterns show that overlap metrics and human ratings answer different questions: overlap metrics measure how \emph{similar} a rewrite is to its source, while human judgment evaluates how \emph{good} the rewrite is.
The critical insight is that these are not merely different metrics; they are \emph{inversely correlated} for rewriting.
Pattern~1 shows that high source similarity indicates \emph{insufficient transformation} (the rewrite barely changed anything), while Pattern~2 shows that high source dissimilarity indicates \emph{successful transformation} (the rewrite meaningfully transformed the text).
This inverse relationship explains why the negative correlations are universal and statistically significant rather than reflecting random noise or a specific metric deficiency.
For text rewriting, a task where the goal is to differ from the source while preserving meaning, these two objectives are inversely correlated.
This explains why the strongest negative correlations are observed for character level metrics (JACCARD CHAR: $\rho$=$-$0.60), which most directly measure surface overlap, while semantic metrics like SBERT COSINE ($\rho$=$-$0.28) show somewhat weaker, but still negative, correlations.
